Scam Avoidance: Protect Yourself from AI Scams

Scammers use AI to make fake voices, emails, and websites look real. Here is how to spot them — in plain English.

What you will do

  • Recognize common scam tricks
  • Spot AI-generated fakes (voices, emails, pictures)
  • Know what never to share
  • Know what to do if you think you were scammed

What you need

  • A phone, email, and common sense
  • A trusted family member or friend you can call if something seems off

Part 1: Common scams aimed at retirees

ScamHow it worksRed flags
Grandchild emergency Someone calls pretending to be your grandchild, says they are in trouble, and asks for money right away Urgency, secrecy, asks for gift cards or wire transfer
Tech support A pop-up or call says your computer has a virus and offers to “fix” it You did not call them; they ask for remote access or payment
Romance Someone online professes love quickly, then asks for money Never met in person; stories about emergencies
Medicare/insurance Calls claiming to be from Medicare asking for your Medicare number Medicare will not call you out of the blue
Charity Fake charities after disasters, often with convincing websites Pressure to donate immediately; odd payment methods
AI voice scam AI clones a loved one's voice from a short recording and calls asking for money The voice sounds right, but the request is strange and urgent

Part 2: How to spot AI fakes

AI can now copy voices, write convincing emails, and make realistic pictures. Here is what to look for:

  • The voice sounds like your grandchild, but the story is odd. Hang up and call your grandchild back on their real number.
  • An email looks official but asks you to click a link or call a number. Check the sender's address carefully. Scammers often change one letter (for example, “amaz0n.com” instead of “amazon.com”).
  • A picture or video seems too shocking or urgent. AI images can be made in seconds. Do not act on surprise alone.
  • You are pressured to act NOW. Real organizations give you time to think. Scammers rush you.

Part 3: The golden rules

  1. Never send money to someone you have not met in person.
  2. Never buy gift cards, wire money, or send cryptocurrency because someone asked you to over the phone or email. No real government agency or business does this.
  3. Never give your Social Security number, Medicare number, bank account number, or passwords to someone who calls, emails, or texts you first.
  4. Never let a stranger remote into your computer.
  5. When in doubt, wait and talk to someone you trust.

Part 4: Step-by-step safety checklist

  • Save a “trusted person” contact in your phone — someone you can call before acting on any suspicious request
  • If you get an urgent call about money, say: “Let me call you back,” then hang up and call the person or organization on a number you know is real
  • Never call a phone number that appears in a pop-up warning on your computer
  • Set a voicemail greeting so unknown calls go to voicemail — you can listen first and decide
  • Check charity websites at give.org or charitynavigator.org before donating
  • Review your bank and credit card statements once a month for charges you do not recognize
  • If you think you were scammed, call your bank immediately and report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov

Part 5: What to do if you were scammed

  • Do not feel embarrassed — it can happen to anyone, and scammers are professionals
  • Call your bank or credit card company right away
  • Report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov
  • Tell a trusted family member or friend so they can help you
  • Change any passwords you may have shared

Example: what to say to a suspicious caller

“I do not make decisions over the phone. Please send me something in the mail, or I will call your office on the number I already have for you. Goodbye.”

Then hang up.
